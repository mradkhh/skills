# Motion, optics, type, and sound

Apply these through the chosen storyboard. The goal is the quality of rule 1, not a show of every tool at once.

## Camera and depth

- Treat path, speed, orientation, target, FOV, and focus as separate channels.
- Design one long connected path with waves of speed. Do not chain identical ease-in-out moves that stop dead at every accent.
- At every join, check position, direction, screen speed, and acceleration.
- In a reading moment, a big move settles into a calm arc or drift.
- Following the hero and strong parallax of the surroundings work together.
- Foreground, midground, and background differ in scale, relative motion, overlap, and perspective. Protect silhouettes and text from accidental overlap.
- A label next to a moving 3D object follows the **projection of its anchor**, keeps its margins, and does not jitter or lag.

## Optics and material

- **Motion blur** shows relative screen motion during the exposure. A tracked, sharp hero can have a blurred background. Blur fades as motion brakes. Sub-frame sampling is a means, not proof of the effect.
- **Depth of field** isolates the chosen depth. On a push-in, focus follows the meaningful detail. Near and far layers may go soft; text and the hero stay readable. Do not blur a flat UI just to tick a box.
- **Radial / zoom blur** is a stylised accent for depth or fast cards. It is not DOF and not motion blur. Never put maximum blur on the whole video.
- **Form reads from** geometry, light, normals, thickness, contact shadow, and overlap. Highlights, grain, and particles do not fix wrong construction. Texture and shadow follow the object and the light.

## Type and rhythm

- Choose an expressive, available typeface or a matched pair. Check the exact glyphs you need (Cyrillic, Uzbek `o‘ g‘`), weights, line breaks, and the font's license. When external fonts are not allowed, use system fonts or draw the letters.
- Design the readable final layout first, then the action of letters and words: assembly, width change, masks, spatial relations, interaction with objects. Each effect has a job in the story.
- Build actions from anticipation, action, contact, reaction, and settle — with different masses and curves.
- One main accent at a time, with secondary layers in support. When everything fights for attention, shift phase, scale, depth, or contrast.
- Do not fix pacing by cutting required elements or speeding up past legibility. Repeated flashes are not energy.

## Music and sound design

Unless the brief says silent, music and SFX are part of the piece from the start. Pick the method by what is allowed and what fits; the musical result matters, not the tool.

- Music needs a motif or groove, development, contrast, and an ending. A pile of whooshes and hits is not a composition.
- Tie SFX to motion, contact, material, and transitions — not at random.
- Sound and picture follow the same events. After a speed ramp, recompute where the accent lands; do not shift the whole tempo or pitch by accident.
- Listen to the full mix and the stems you need. Check balance, clipping, clicks, sync, and the tail. Metrics do not prove musical quality. Say clearly when audio was not made, or made but not heard.

### Cutting on accents

Plan musical moments together with the animation: a featured note, a hit, a phrase or harmony change, a pause, the return of the beat can carry a cut, a reveal, a camera change, a contact, or a landing. Use it periodically and with meaning — not a cut on every beat.

Once the **actual track** exists:

1. Mark the timecodes of the clear accents and the visual events they should carry.
2. For each pick, note the perceived peak or onset, the visual target, and the nearest frame: `frame / fps`. Account for the real audio start, offsets, and edits.
3. Same BPM, a computed grid, or the start of an SFX file is not enough: the musical peak, the attack, and the visual climax can sit elsewhere. Check by ear and in motion, to the frame — not from a waveform alone.

When a sync does not work, gently adjust shot length, path, reveal or landing timing, or the arrangement, within the brief. Meaning, reading time, voice, and an explicit silent brief all outrank hitting the grid.

Let SFX support, complement, or leave room for a musical accent. Do not stack the same whoosh on every cut or double every hit. After you replace or trim the track, or add a speed ramp, update the accent map. Never claim perfect sync without watching and listening to it.
